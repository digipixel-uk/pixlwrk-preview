2c:T1269,<p class="article-opening">A website can feel old long before it becomes technically unusable. That is why “we need a new website” is often the beginning of the conversation rather than the conclusion.</p>
<p>Sometimes the right answer is a complete rebuild. Sometimes it is a new content structure on the existing platform. Sometimes a design refresh, better service pages and clearer calls to action will produce more value than replacing everything underneath.</p>
<h2 id="the-first-question-is-what-is-actually-failing">The first question is: what is actually failing?</h2>
<p>Separate the symptoms before discussing platforms or visual concepts. A website problem usually sits in one or more of four areas: proposition, structure, presentation and technology.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Proposition:</strong> visitors cannot quickly understand what the business offers or why it is relevant.</li><li><strong>Structure:</strong> the right information exists, but the navigation and page hierarchy make it difficult to find.</li><li><strong>Presentation:</strong> the experience feels dated, inconsistent or weak on mobile.</li><li><strong>Technology:</strong> the platform is slow, insecure, difficult to edit or unable to support integrations and future requirements.</li></ul>
<p>If most of the pain is in the first three areas, replacing the CMS may add cost without solving the real issue. If the technology itself is preventing sensible change, a rebuild becomes easier to justify.</p>
<h2 id="look-at-the-customer-journey-before-the-sitemap">Look at the customer journey before the sitemap</h2>
<p>A rebuild should not simply recreate the current site with a cleaner visual design. Start with the people arriving, the questions they have and the action you want them to take.</p>
<p>For a service business that might mean moving from broad “Services” pages to specific landing pages that match how people search and buy. For an ecommerce business it may mean improving product discovery, trust signals and checkout. For a membership organisation it could mean making recurring tasks easier for existing users rather than focusing only on acquisition.</p>
<h3 id="useful-evidence-to-review">Useful evidence to review</h3>
<ul><li>Which pages attract organic search traffic?</li><li>Where do paid campaigns currently send people?</li><li>Which pages produce enquiries or sales?</li><li>What do prospects repeatedly ask before buying?</li><li>Which content is hard for the team to update?</li><li>Which technical limitations keep delaying useful changes?</li></ul>
<h2 id="when-a-redesign-is-probably-enough">When a redesign is probably enough</h2>
<p>A redesign can be the sensible route when the underlying platform is healthy, content can be managed properly and the core information architecture still works. In that situation, changing templates, typography, spacing, imagery, calls to action and page composition can materially improve the experience without introducing an unnecessary migration.</p>
<p>It is particularly attractive when there is a strong body of existing search content that you do not want to disrupt.</p>
<h2 id="when-a-rebuild-is-easier-to-justify">When a rebuild is easier to justify</h2>
<p>A rebuild becomes more compelling when the existing platform creates persistent constraints: poor performance that cannot be addressed sensibly, fragile plugins or custom code, a difficult editing experience, inaccessible templates, broken mobile behaviour, weak integration options or an architecture that no longer matches the business.</p>
<p>The important distinction is that the rebuild is solving a defined constraint. “A new website will feel fresher” is a weak business case on its own.</p>
<h2 id="protect-what-already-works">Protect what already works</h2>
<p>One of the easiest mistakes during a rebuild is to throw away useful search visibility, URLs, content and conversion behaviour simply because the old site looks untidy.</p>
<p>Before launch, map existing URLs, preserve valuable content, plan redirects, benchmark performance and ensure analytics can compare old and new. A visual improvement is not a success if qualified traffic disappears after launch.</p>
<h2 id="choose-the-smallest-change-that-solves-the-real-problem">Choose the smallest change that solves the real problem</h2>
<p>The best project is not automatically the biggest one. A good website decision creates a clearer customer journey, removes real technical constraints and leaves the business with something it can maintain and improve.</p>
<p>If that requires a rebuild, rebuild it properly.
